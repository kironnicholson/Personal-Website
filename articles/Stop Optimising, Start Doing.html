Stop Optimising, Start Doing

Published: 11 November 2026

I recently discovered that I spend a lot of time doing meta-work.

What is meta-work?

Meta-work is “work that makes work possible.” It covers the planning, administrative tasks, and related activities required to execute a project — without actually producing the end product.

My problem

If you’re anything like me, you’re all about optimisation. You get stuck in an endless loop of researching, spending perhaps 50–60% of your time trying to find the “best” solution.

The big issue is this: time spent thinking about and researching the optimum solution is time not spent solving the problem.

Action is the only thing that produces real, tangible progress.

You might be asking…

…if I’m telling you to go gung-ho and just get straight to work.

Obviously not. Planning, admin, and the work you don’t particularly want to do are required for almost every task.

What I’m pointing out is that meta-work should be limited.

Time spent learning about which skills to learn, and what to do, is time not spent in the trenches building depth in those skills.

Here’s the research

Asana’s Anatomy of Work Index shows that the average knowledge worker spends about 58–60% of their time on meta-work (or “work about work”), leaving less time for skilled or craft work. The average person estimates it at only 35%. Independent Microsoft studies put the figure at 53–61% on coordination versus creation.

Hubstaff’s tracking of large numbers of workers shows an average deep-work focus time of 2–3 hours per day. This is largely due to the fragmentation of focus by multiple noise sources.

Academic work emphasises that meta-work is necessary but often under-recognised. However, excessive meta-work creates measurable drag through delayed projects, duplicated effort, and reduced time for high-value output. Context switching can consume up to 40% of productive capacity in some studies.

Benefits of appropriate meta-work

  • Enables efficiency in primary work through better prioritisation, resource gathering, and alignment
  • Supports collaboration and visibility, particularly in distributed settings
  • Builds systems that compound, such as decision records and clear documentation
  • Aids directionality and quality control when AI is heavily used
  • Prevents chaos, project drift, and duplicated effort

Drawbacks of excessive meta-work

  • Directly reduces time spent on skilled and strategic work, leading to lower output and missed deadlines
  • Contributes to stress, burnout, apathy, and disengagement
  • Creates an illusion of progress with little to no real, tangible value creation
  • Amplifies context-switching costs, productivity loss, and fragmentation
  • Is exceptionally costly when low-quality (status meetings without decisions, information hunting, duplicated effort)

Drawbacks of insufficient meta-work

  • Poor coordination, higher risk of misalignment, and lack of shared context
  • Increased rework, duplicated effort, and lost information
  • Difficulty scaling work and adapting to changing conditions
  • Higher long-term friction: repeated process redesign, inefficient resource allocation, and unrecorded decisions with no “why”
  • Late-stage firefighting

Consensus

There is no universal consensus on how much time should be spent on meta-work. Use the following as practical guidelines:

  • Individual contributors and pure makers should aim for 15–30%, with beginners tending toward the higher end
  • Managers and coordinators should aim for 40–60%, while still protecting enough time for strategic work

Protecting meaningful daily blocks for deep focus is crucial. To do the work is to move the needle.

An attainable goal is to define sufficient, high-quality, “good-enough” meta-work rather than maximising or minimising the category in absolute terms. Periodic audits of how you spend your time and what fills your calendar can help you find the right balance for your role.

Hope this helps.
